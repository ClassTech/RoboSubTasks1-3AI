Summary

The sub starts facing any of three directions in front of a gate split down the middle. It must go through one side, slalom through three sets of poles on that same side, turn around, and come back the same way: through the three sets in the same lanes and the gate's same opening. Right after the gate it can earn style points with a victory dance: a full turn each way, stopping at every quarter, then a full roll each way.

A scripted mission, ported from ClassTech's RoboSim, is the baseline. The learned mission uses four skills: gate, slalom (all three sets) and turnaround, trained by reinforcement learning (PPO), and the dance, trained by imitation. A runner that only says what to do runs them in six steps. On 300 test starts, at the sub's real thrust, without the dance (the scripted mission has none):

MissionSuccess, 300 startsTime (median)
Scripted281 (93.7%)~134 s
Learned289 (96.3%)~119 s

With the dance, on another 300 starts, the learned mission succeeds 287 times against 286 without it, in about 224 s, and 234 of the dances (78%) are complete. The dance's points are extra, so a dance cut short doesn't fail the run. The skills, each judged on its own:

SkillJobSuccess on its test starts
Gatefind the gate from any heading, go through the commanded side; also the way back91% of 100
Slalomevery set ahead in the commanded lane, then clear of the last88% of 100
Turnaroundafter set 3, turn back to face it, far enough off to see the lane90% of 100
Danceeight quarter turns with 3 s stops, eight quarter rolls, level again81% of 225 (14.3 of 16 stops on average)

Everything carries over from the qualification project: the realistic simulator, the sensing and control code, PPO training, and these ideas:

  1. Train only on what the real sub can sense. The policies see camera boxes, depth, heading, pitch and turn rates, never the simulator's true position or speed.
  2. The policy sets targets; the sub's own control loops fly them. Ten times a second it picks a turn, a depth and a forward thrust.
  3. Skip the rendering when training. A simulated detector computes the boxes the camera would see.
  4. Make the simulation hard on purpose. Noisy detections, a 0.5 s thruster ramp, and dynamics varied every run.
  5. The runner says what to do, not how. It names a skill and a side; each skill decides how, and a learned done detector says when it's finished. No scripted stages or maneuvers live in the runner.
  6. When exploring can't find the reward, show it. PPO alone never got the dance past its first stop; imitating a hand controller that could see the judges' count, it learned the whole dance in a few hours.

The big picture

The policy never drives the thrusters directly. Ten times a second it reads the observation and picks targets; the sub's ordinary control loops hold those targets 60 times a second.

Two ways fly the course, sharing the physics, sensors, detection, perception and control code, so comparisons are fair:

  • The scripted mission (tasks_mission.py, ai/tasks/course_tasks.py): RoboSim's hand-written tasks, adapted to this sub. It is the baseline.
  • The learned mission (TasksMissionRunner in tasks_mission.py): the gate, dance, slalom and turnaround skills in turn, each until it says it's finished.

The course

The course follows ClassTech's RoboSim (the 2025 tasks 1–2 simulator); where in doubt, RoboSim decides. It lives as data in course.py: a list of segments (poles and bars, each with a radius and a color) and pass lines with named lanes. Collisions, the camera and top views, and the simulated detector all build from the same list.

Sides are relative to the sub. Going out, "left" is the course's left; coming back, the same physical lane is on the sub's right. Each skill takes the side as an input, so the runner tells it "left" or "right" as the sub sees it.

The simulator

The simulator (sim_core.py) advances the pool, the course and the sub in fixed 1/60 s steps, with no graphics. Training, scoring and the pygame window all drive this same core. The pool is 40 m long (x), 15 m wide (y) and 2.1 m deep (z, positive down).

The thrusters

Each corner thruster is angled across the diagonal, so it pushes toward the far side and turns the sub away from its own side. The front-left one, running forward, pushes forward and to the right and turns the sub right; the front-right one pushes forward-left and turns it left. The rear pair is the other way round for sliding: rear-left pushes forward-left. Running all four forward cancels the sideways pushes and the turns.

The two vertical thrusters, one on each side, move the sub up and down together and roll it when pushed apart (right side down is positive roll). On its side, the vertical pair pushes sideways, so the depth loop also uses the corner thrusters, whose sideways push points up or down when the sub is rolled (see Low-level control).

Until October 24 the simulator and the mixer had every horizontal thruster mirrored left for right. They agreed with each other, so nothing flew differently (the forces match to 10⁻¹⁵ and the trained gate policy scores the same), but on the real sub the names choose which motor runs.

Collisions and passes

The sub is a box turned to its heading; every course segment is a line with a radius. On contact the sub returns to its last clear position and stops. Every crossing of a pass line (the gate, each slalom set) is recorded with its direction, its lane (or none, if outside them), and its depth: a lane only counts within the poles' vertical span.

Varied dynamics

In training each run scales the PID gains and inertia by 0.7–1.3, each drag coefficient by 0.8–1.2 and the thrust by 0.9–1.1. The dance's training also scales the roll righting moment by 0.5–1.5, since it is a guess. Scored tests use the nominal values unless marked "varied".

Sensing

The sub has a forward camera, a pressure sensor and an IMU with a magnetometer; nothing measures its horizontal speed or position.

SensorSimulated as
Pressure → depthtrue depth + 3 mm noise + a zero error of up to ±2 cm per run
Magnetometer → headingtrue heading + 0.5° noise + an offset of up to ±15° per run (steel in the pool)
IMU → pitch, roll, ratestrue values + small noise; gyro bias up to ±0.005 rad/s per run

The detector returns red, black and white boxes. Its noise is drawn per run: centers shifted a few pixels, sizes off 2–10%, dropouts of about 0.3 s, one-frame phantom boxes (only in the course's colors), and 30–150 ms latency. OpenCV and the simulated detector agree, apart from a wide, flat dark patch in the background photo and far white poles below the simulated detector's minimum size; neither is pole-shaped enough to matter.

Perception and the observation

From boxes to objects (tasks_perception.py)

  • Gate poles are a red box and a black box stacked end to end. The color order says which physical pole it is (red over black is the course's right pole) from either side, so the return can't mix them up.
  • The divider is the red box between the poles, level with their tops. It is found, then kept out of the slalom's search.
  • Gate range comes from the angle down to a pole's foot on the floor; slalom range from the known 0.9 m pole height (the floating poles have no foot on the floor).
  • A slalom set is a red pole with a white at about the same range. Lone reds are the gate's; requiring a white stopped the gate's reds and the divider from posing as phantom sets.
  • Passages and lanes are small gates: the same geometry code as the whole gate, on their two edges, gives the turn to face straight through and the sideways offset.

Checked against ground truth (tools/check_tasks_perception.py): with the perfect detector every gate pole is identified correctly, the gate's angle is right to 0.4° and its offset to 5 cm. With noise, the gate's offset is about 0.7 m off per frame and 0.17 m once smoothed over 1 s. A lane is only 1.52 m wide, so its offset stays rough (about 0.26 m smoothed), but its bearing, from image positions alone, is precise to under 1°.

Memory and confirmation

  • When something drops out of view its last values are kept, with "found" at 0 and the time since seen counting up. Bearings are corrected for how far the sub has turned since, not for how far it has moved.
  • Gate poles are tracked by physical identity but shown as left and right the way the sub sees them now.
  • Sightings must be confirmed: 3 frames in a row for poles and sets, 2 for the whole gate and its passages. At 3, the gate needed both poles in one frame, and each one-frame dropout cost 4 frames: it was confirmed only 42% of the time it was in view.
  • Geometry (turn and offset) is smoothed over about 1 s.
  • Dead reckoning (slalom and turnaround): metres driven since the red pole was last in view, summed from the sub's own surge commands at the sim's top speed (0.51 m/s; the real sub's hasn't been measured). Close to a set its poles leave the view, and the remembered range doesn't shrink as the sub moves, so before this nothing changed while it crossed blind. With the red's last range, it says how far through the set the sub is. It covers only a few seconds and metres, so its errors stay small. The thruster ramp, currents and sideways drift aren't in it.
  • Which set: the slalom's observation describes one set at a time. A set more than 2 m farther than the tracked one is ignored until dead reckoning says the sub has driven past the tracked one. Without this, when the near set's outer white left the view (a set needs a white), the next set, 4 m on, became "the nearest set" and the policy steered for its lane, into the near set's white.

Low-level control

The control loops (controller.py) are shared by the scripted mission and the policies, and are written to run on the Pi unchanged.

  • Depth: PID on measured depth (P 4.5, I 0.05, D 1.0). The target moves at most 0.5 m/s. Retuned at the real thrust, where the old gains (P 9, I 0.1, D 3) rang for about 20 s; a 0.5 m step now settles in about 1.8 s.
  • Heading: PD toward the target heading, using the gyro (0.1 per degree, 4.0 per rad/s). The target moves at most 30°/s; a policy's turn command is up to ±45° from the current heading. At the real thrust, holding a heading wobbles up to 14°/s; the dance halves both gains (back to about 6°/s, turning as fast). The other skills keep the defaults: on the halved gains, outside their training spread, the mission lost 11 of 180.
  • Pitch: not controlled; the vertical thrusters are on the sides and the sub is stable on its own.
  • Roll: open loop. A roll effort pushes the vertical pair apart; with none, the sub rights itself. Roll has first call on the vertical pair: sharing it, a saturated depth loop halved the roll torque and the sub stalled partway round. Rolled past 20°, the depth loop's push is split between the vertical pair (× cos roll) and the corner thrusters' sideways push (× −sin roll); with the vertical pair alone, the buoyant sub rose to the surface during the rolls.
  • Mixer: forward, sideways, yaw, heave and roll efforts into the six thrusters. The horizontal group is scaled down on its own if any command exceeds 1, so hard turning can't steal vertical thrust.

Each skill limits its depth targets to keep clear of the course: the gate 1.25–1.65 m (below the top bar, at most 1.1 m deep, and high enough above the floor to range the pole feet), the slalom and turnaround 1.0–1.45 m (within the floating poles' span), the dance 1.0–1.8 m (it rolls deep: rolling uses all the vertical thrust and the sub rises about 0.1 m/s).

One SIM ONLY helper in the scripted tasks damps sideways motion using the simulator's true velocity, which the real sub can't measure. It must turn that velocity by the true heading: turned by the measured one, off up to 15°, it read forward speed as sideways and pushed the sub off its line.

The neural networks

Each skill has two small networks, an actor (the policy) and a critic (its estimate of the reward to come), each with two hidden layers of 128 tanh neurons. Only the actors would run on the sub.

When the dead-reckoning input was added, the trained slalom and turnaround were carried over rather than retrained (tools/expand_slalom_obs.py): the new input's weights start at zero, so the copy flies exactly as the original (actions within 10⁻⁶), and fine-tuning learns to use it. Trained from scratch on the new observation, the slalom reached only 37–57% in 4M steps.

In training, each action is drawn from a bell curve around the actor's output; when flying, the output itself is used, clipped to ±1. None of these skills has sway (sideways thrust): with it, the first gate policies slid through crabwise (see the gate skill).

A worked example of one decision, from the qualification project's gate policy: it output turn −0.54 and surge +3.30 (clipped to +1). That became a 24° turn to the right at full ahead. The mixer then ran the left-side thrusters at full and the right-side ones at 0.42; the stronger left side turns the sub right.

How training works

Training (train.py) uses PPO from Stable-Baselines3: fly the current policy to collect experience, judge each action against the critic's expectation, nudge both networks, and repeat. Two seeds train side by side, 9 simulators each.

  1. Collect. About 8,190 steps (910 per simulator), each recording the observation, action, its probability, the critic's estimate and the reward.
  2. Score. Each step's advantage: how much better things went than the critic expected, counting the steps that follow, discounted (γ = 0.995, λ = 0.95).
  3. Update. 10 passes in chunks of 256, each action's probability moving at most about 20%, stopping early once the policy has moved far enough (target_kl 0.03).

TensorBoard (.venv/bin/tensorboard --logdir runs, then http://localhost:6006) shows each run's curves; eval/success_rate is the headline. The training log prints each test's outcomes. The turnaround took about 20 minutes for 2 million steps; the gate and slalom 4 million steps from scratch, then fine-tuning. When the thrust was corrected (October 28) every skill and done detector was fine-tuned from its best at the old thrust. The dance is trained differently, by imitation (see The victory dance).

The scripted mission

build_tasks_mission() ports RoboSim's mission: gate, stabilize 3 s, slalom, stabilize 3 s, slalom reversed, gate, hover 10 s, surface. It has no dance. The tasks are in ai/tasks/course_tasks.py. Run it with .venv/bin/python main.py --course tasks --detector noisy.

The side is chosen at the first sight of the gate: the passage nearer the image center. The learned mission will use the same rule.

The skills

Each skill is a Gymnasium environment built on the shared base (sub_env.py). It places the sub, runs the control loop, decides how an episode ends, and pays reward from ground truth (only in training; it never reaches the sub). Every skill ends on a collision, on surfacing, or at a time limit, and takes the side to go through as an input.

Gate (tasks_gate_env.py)

Go through the commanded passage and carry on 1.5 m beyond, within 90 s.

  • Starts: forward, 3–10 m before the gate, half at RoboSim's start headings and half at any heading, so finding the gate is part of the skill; or (40%) the return. Half the returns start as the slalom hands over: about 6 m out near the lane's line, facing back within 20°, moving 0.2–0.5 m/s; the rest 2–7 m past the gate at rest. Up to ±2 m sideways, 0.5–1.5 m deep. The way back was the gate's weak spot until its starts matched the slalom's handoff.
  • No sway. With it, from scratch, the policies went through crabwise, about 52° off, and hit the divider they couldn't see (19 of 25 collisions). Turned 45°, the square sub is 0.65 m across, in a passage with about 0.5 m to spare each side. A cost on facing changed nothing; taking sway away took the gate from 70% to 90%. Sliding through is legal in competition, but this sub can't see what it slides into.

Slalom (tasks_slalom_env.py)

Go through every set ahead, in the commanded lane, and carry on 1.5 m beyond the last, within 120 s. The mission runs it once each way.

  • One skill for the whole slalom (the user's call). It began as one set per skill, run three times each way. Then every handoff between sets came from the previous step's done detector: the sub was moving, at whatever moment the detector fired. Single-set training never saw that, and a skill that scored about 90% on one set lost about a quarter of the missions. Now the switch between sets happens inside the skill and gets trained with it.
  • Starts: 30% as the gate hands over and about 20% as the turnaround hands over, all flown in (lead-ins): the gate skill flies from RoboSim's start, or the slalom and turnaround fly the way out and turn, and the slalom takes over where that step's detector fires, moving, with the memory it really has. The rest start at rest 1.5–3.5 m before any set, near the lane's line and facing it within 30°. Placed starts didn't match the mission: from placed back starts (at rest, lined up) the slalom crossed set 3 and straight back again, and from placed gate starts it recrossed the gate after early handoffs.
  • Carries on 3 s past the finish. The detector can fire a little late, and in the mission half the failures were the sub turning back across a set it had just passed, in seconds training never reached. Crossing back in those 3 s fails.
  • Any other line fails: a set already passed, one out of order, or the gate. It used to be ignored, and with the set just passed 1.5 m behind, the policy's habit of backing up first took it back through that set.
  • Backwards fails. When turnaround starts were part of this skill, a policy learned to reverse blind through set 3, picking a lane by luck. Crossing the set's line facing more than 90° from the way through now fails. Backing through would count in competition, but with one forward camera it is always blind.

Turnaround (tasks_turnaround_env.py)

After the forward slalom, turn back to face set 3 from far enough off to see the lane, so the slalom skill can take it from there. Within 45 s.

  • Why its own skill: as part of the slalom (30–50% of its starts), the slalom turned around only 8–14% of the time, and got worse at everything else (88% → 73%). A lane is a red and a white pole 1.52 m apart; both fit in the 70° view only from at least 2.2 m off, so from closer the sub had to turn, back off and line up mostly blind.
  • Starts: 75% begin with a lead-in. The slalom skill flies the whole slalom out from where the gate hands over, and the turnaround takes over when the slalom's detector fires, with the memory, speed and position the mission really has. The rest start 1–3.5 m past set 3, facing on (away from it), with the memory filled from ground truth. That ground-truth fill was the first version, and it didn't match the mission. It used the set's current range, seen 2 s ago, while the mission leaves the range when last seen (about 1 m) from 5–10 s before. There the turnaround's detector fired at once, still facing away. A lead-in through set 3 alone wasn't right either: after the whole slalom, the turnaround drove off without turning.
  • Done (ground truth): within 0.75 m of a point 3 m back from the set in front of the commanded lane, facing straight back within 15°, with the red pole and our white pole both in view.
  • Going through the set fails: that's the slalom's job.

The victory dance

RoboSim's dance, between the gate and the slalom, scores a point for each 90° turn in any axis and direction. The user asked for it learned, not programmed. It lives in tasks_dance_env.py; the teacher and the imitation training are in tools/imitate_dance.py.

What it does, and how it's judged

  1. Yaw: a full turn clockwise, stopping at each quarter, then a full turn back counterclockwise: eight stops. A stop counts once the sub is within 5° of it and turning slower than 5°/s for 3 s, measured on the true heading from where the dance began (so errors don't add up from stop to stop, and the magnetometer's offset cancels).
  2. Roll: a full roll right (right side down), then a full roll back left: eight quarters, each counted on reaching it within 15°. There's no hold: past about 60° the side thrusters can't hold the sub there, and on its side or upside down they push it sideways while the buoyant sub rises.
  3. Level: the last quarter, level again, held 2 s under 10°/s.

Judging (DanceJudge) runs every physics step on ground truth, as the judges would see it. The mission env runs the same judge from the moment the runner starts the dance and reports dance_stops (0–16). The dance is extra points: a dance cut short doesn't fail the mission.

Actions (DanceDriver), at 10 Hz

  • Turn slews a turn target, measured from the dance's starting heading. Past ±0.3 it moves at up to 60°/s; inside, it snaps to a quarter: on to the next one if it has moved more than 15° that way, otherwise back. One push moves it at most a quarter. Jitter averages out and holding a stop is easy; turning on is a deliberate push.
  • Depth, 1.0–1.8 m: deep before rolling.
  • Roll: past ±0.4, open-loop roll effort; inside, a small PD on the IMU's roll holds it level.

No surge: exploring with it shook the sub off its stops (see below). The driver also halves the heading gains, as the dance trained.

The observation (DanceObservation, 15 per frame, 4 frames)

All measured: depth and depth rate, pitch, yaw rate; heading turned since the dance began and the farthest clockwise so far (−180° on the way out and on the way back look alike without it); the turn target; how long it has held the target, by its own count (within 3° and under 3°/s, stricter than the judges' 5° and 5°/s); a stop_done flag, set 0.5 s after its own count passes the judges' 3 s; sin and cos of roll, roll rate, roll since the start and the most so far; time level. No last action, unlike the other skills.

Why imitation

About 300 CPU-hours of PPO, over nine designs, never got past the first stop. Each design fixed a real problem, found by tracing a run:

TriedWhat happened
Turn target anywhere in 440°Exploring moved it ±80° a step; a stop needs 5° for 3 s, so none was ever held and there was nothing to learn from.
Quarter steps fired by the action crossing ±0.5Exploring fired them early in a hold and lost the stop, so the policy learned never to fire.
Starts at any stop; "still" counted as the judges count itIt held the stop it started at and never turned on: turning on pays only just after a stop counts, and early it loses the stop.
Slewed target; training on 2-stop piecesTraining success reached about 40% on the pieces, but the whole dance stayed lost.
The causeExploring's noise is a physical shake. The hand controller, with the training noise added to its actions, held only 9 of 30 two-stop pieces (95% clean). Surge was the worst, so it was dropped.

Imitation (DAgger): a hand controller that can see the judges' count flies first, with noise on its actions so the data has recoveries in it. Then the student flies, with a little noise, and the teacher labels every step, so the student learns to recover from its own mistakes. After each round the policy's mean action is fitted to the teacher's on everything so far. Eight rounds took about 2.5 hours.

Fixes on the way, each from a trace of the student:

  • No contradictory labels. The teacher moved on the moment the judges counted a stop, a step or two before the student could tell; the same observation got "hold" and "push", and the student pushed at half strength. The stop_done flag, and a teacher that waits for it, fixed that (to 40%).
  • The policy's own count stricter than the judges'. The judges see rate spikes between the 10 Hz gyro readings, so a quarter of the holds the student counted weren't theirs, and it moved on too early. Counting at 3° and 3°/s: 40% → 78%.
  • No copying itself. With its own last action in the observation, the student copied it, and flew into states it never trained on.
  • Commit and stop. A push past 15° commits to the next quarter (half-strength pushes kept snapping back), and one push moves at most a quarter (pushes ran through stops).

PPO fine-tuning from the imitated policy (two learning rates, 3M steps) didn't improve it: on 225 fresh starts, 77% and 65% against the imitated policy's 81%. The dance is tdance_bc8: 81% of 225 dances, 14.3 of 16 stops on average, about 101 s. Failures: about 10% lost (the sub's own count went on past a stop the judges didn't count) and 10% out of time (the 120 s limit).

Reward weights

The reward is the only way to tell a policy what matters, and policies find every loophole: "you get what you ask for". Every weight below either encourages a behavior or closes a loophole a policy found.

How the pieces fit

  • Progress is a change in a score, not a payment per step. Each skill defines a ground-truth score of how good the current position is; each step pays its change. The total depends only on where an episode starts and ends, so swimming back and forth earns nothing.
  • The big payoff is the job itself, larger than all the shaping combined.
  • Any failure costs the same −5, timing out included, so hovering safely never beats trying.
  • Only relative sizes matter. Training rescales rewards by their running spread.

The mission runner and done detectors

The runner (TasksMissionRunner in tasks_mission.py) says what to do, never how. It's a plain list of six (skill, side) steps, each flown by its policy until that skill's done detector says it's finished; then it holds depth and heading. There are no scripted stages, maneuvers or switching rules. Each step starts its skill with a fresh observation, as in training, except the turnaround, which keeps the slalom's memory of set 3.

Done detectors (done_detector.py, tools/train_done_detector.py)

As in the qualification project: a small network (128-128) reading the policy's own observation, trained by supervised learning on 800–1,500 flights of the skill's own policy (the gate's half from the mission's own starts, the slalom's half from lead-ins), each moment labeled with ground truth (through and 1.5 m beyond: the gate, or the slalom's last set; placed and facing, for the turnaround). numpy only, so it runs on the Pi.

Close to a gate or a set, the poles leave the 70° view, so the last meter before the line and the meters after it look alike. The first gate and slalom detectors fired 2.5–7 s early; the slalom's, about 2 m short of set 1, handed the next slalom step the same set again. They now also get a short history, all measured: the target's closest range while in view, the time since, and roughly how far the sub has driven since (its own surge commands added up). Per-moment accuracy went from 89% to 97% (slalom) and 91% to 96% (gate). Settings: 3 steps in a row above 0.5 (gate, turnaround) or 0.7 (slalom, with the moments before the pass weighted ×3 in training, because firing early breaks the mission while firing late doesn't).

Handoffs

Most of the work after the runner was making each skill's training starts match what the step before it really hands over:

  • Turnaround: lead-in starts (see the skill). From ground-truth memory: its detector fired at once in the mission. From a lead-in through set 3: 67%, then 94% after fine-tuning. From a lead-in through the whole slalom, needed once the slalom became one skill: 80%, then 96% fine-tuned on the noisy detector with the white poles' stale ranges redrawn (--scramble-starts), and retrained on each new slalom's handoffs since.
  • Slalom back and gate back: slalom back starts flown in through the whole slalom and the turnaround (recrossing set 3 coming back went from 6 to 0 in 180 missions); gate returns started as the slalom hands over, moving, about 6 m out (the gate's failures, the most of any step, fell to 1–2).
  • Slalom, while it was one set per step: lead-in starts that flew the set before and handed over the same way. Episodes carried on 3 s past the finish, and crossing another line failed. With those, the nine-step mission reached 128/180. Making the slalom one skill removed four of the five handoffs instead.
  • Perception: one set at a time (see the observation). Fixing that took the one-set slalom from 79% to 90% on its training starts without retraining.

Tried on the way, and not kept

  • Time since seen counting to 15 s (instead of 5) for the slalom: fine-tuned on it, the nine-step mission fell to 33–52%.
  • Dead reckoning with one-set training: the policy barely used the new input (its weights grew to about 0.1, against 1.7 for the rest), and the mission did no better. Hovering on the line was rare in one-set training, so training had little reason to learn the input. That pointed to training where the failures happen: the whole slalom.
  • A gate detector weighted against firing early: no better (8 early handoffs against 6). Training it on the mission's own starts worked instead.
  • The halved heading gains for every skill: smoother, but outside the skills' training spread; the mission lost 11 of 180. Only the dance uses them.
  • The dance by PPO alone (nine designs, about 300 CPU-hours), and PPO fine-tuning of the imitated dance (no better on 225 starts). See The victory dance.

Lessons and design decisions

You get what you ask for. As with an incentive scheme for people, a policy does exactly what it's paid for. Here: crabwise passes through the gate, shaving the red pole, reversing blind through a set. Each was found by asking what the policy was paid for and what it could actually see.

Take away the action, not just reward against it. A facing cost didn't stop the crabwise gate passes; removing sway did, and the skill got better, not worse.

Split a skill that has two jobs. Turning around was a rare, hard start inside the slalom skill: it was learned last and dragged the rest down. As its own small skill it took 20 minutes and reached 97%.

The runner says what, not how. A two-step gate (line up, then drive) was considered and set aside, because it would have put the how into the runner. Problems get fixed in training (rewards, starts, actions) or perception.

Check what the camera can actually see. A lane needs two poles 1.52 m apart in a 70° view, so it is invisible from closer than 2.2 m. That geometry, not the policy, was why turnarounds started too close kept failing.

Make the training starts match the mission, and test from the mission's handoffs. Slalom starts facing away anywhere but set 3 faced another set; starts up to 5 m out put the next set in the way. Neither happens in the mission. And the one handoff the slalom never trained for, 6.5 m out after the gate, was its real weak spot (31/50), hidden inside an average over harder starts. A first test of it was itself wrong (it put other sets in the way); check that a test's starts are the ones meant.

Check that two seeds really differ. Resuming from a checkpoint restored its own random seed, so every "two-seed" fine-tune was one run twice. Identical test results on both gave it away.

SIM ONLY helpers need the truth, all of it. True velocity turned by the measured heading is wrong by the magnetometer's error.

Train each skill from what the step before really hands over. Skills that scored 90–100% from their training starts failed much more often in the mission, handed over moving, at whatever moment the previous detector fired, with memory the previous skill left. Starting training episodes by actually flying the step before (a lead-in) fixed the turnaround and most of the slalom. Then check what the new starts make possible: with the set just passed 1.5 m behind and crossing it ignored, the slalom learned to back through it.

A detector needs to see what changes. 0.4 s of observation can't tell "1 m before the set, poles out of view" from "1 m past it". A little measured history (closest range, time and distance driven since) can.

A skill's own test doesn't predict the mission. One-set slalom policies that scored 90% on their own tests lost more missions than the one they replaced. Chaining six handoffs mattered more than any single set. Judge changes in the mission, and when a failure only appears there, train where it happens: here, by making the whole slalom one skill.

Give the policy what it's missing, then check it uses it. Blind near a set, nothing in the observation changed as the sub crept forward, so it dithered. Dead reckoning (its own surge commands added up) fills that gap. But a new input only helps if training needs it: on one-set episodes its weights stayed near zero. Reading the first layer's weights showed that at once.

Carry trained policies across an observation change. Adding an input with zero weights keeps the policy exactly as it was and lets fine-tuning take it from there; training from scratch threw away weeks of shaping (37–57% after 4M steps).

"Best" on 30 test starts is noisy. Both whole-slalom runs saved their "best" checkpoint at 200k steps, from a lucky 97%. The mission ranked checkpoints differently again.

Seeds matter, and so does the sample. Two fine-tunes from the same checkpoint scored 50/60 and 36/60 in the mission; one policy scored 17/20, then 40/60. Score missions on many starts before believing a change.

Names matter on the real sub. The thruster mirroring never showed in simulation, because the sim and the mixer agreed. Only checking the doc's account of one decision against the real sub's geometry found it.

Check the units. The thrusters were 0.8 kgf, not 0.8 lbf: 2.2 times the thrust. Every skill and the scripted mission had to be retuned, and the depth loop, tuned for the weaker sub, rang for 20 s.

When exploring can't find the reward, show it. The dance's reward was right (a hand-flown dance earned +45.7, sitting still −14.3), but exploration never found a single full stop to learn from. Nine PPO designs got nowhere; imitating a hand controller learned it in hours.

Exploration noise is a physical shake. Noise on the actions doesn't just try new things; it moves the sub. Adding the training noise to a hand controller showed the dance couldn't be held that way at all, and that surge was the worst of it.

A teacher can see what the student can't; its labels mustn't contradict. The teacher saw the judges' count, the student only its own. Where the two disagreed by a step, one observation got both labels and the student split the difference. Make the student's view of "done" lag the truth, and have the teacher wait for it.

Count more strictly than the judges. They check every physics step; the sub's gyro reads at 10 Hz and misses spikes between readings. A margin on the sub's own count took the dance from 40% to 78%.

Check what each step leaves for the next. The dance itself didn't fail; it left the sub 0.4 m deeper, and the way back, minutes later, failed three times as often. Compare with and without on the same starts to see it.

Carried over from the qualification project: confirm detections over several frames; don't trust ranges the sensors can't support; watch for outputs pinned past their ±1 limit; watch runs, not just scores; learn "when" by supervision, not reward.
